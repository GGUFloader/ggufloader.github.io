AI in Legal Jobs: 7 Tasks Lawyers Can Automate Today

AI won't replace lawyers — but lawyers who use AI will replace those who don't. Here's where to start, with the data behind it.

AI is already doing legal work — the question is who uses it well

Every week, legal teams are quietly handing tedious, high-volume work to AI: reading 400-page contracts, searching thousands of documents, drafting first-pass memos. The tools have matured enough that the bottleneck is no longer capability — it's knowing which tasks to automate and which app to trust with confidential client data.

This guide breaks down the 7 legal tasks AI can automate today, what each looks like in practice, and the recommended app for getting them done privately.

Legal AI adoption has already crossed the chasm

This isn't a speculative trend. The American Bar Association's 2024 Legal Technology Survey found overall lawyer adoption of AI-based tools jumped from 11% in 2023 to 30.2% in 2024 — and at firms with 100+ attorneys it nearly tripled to 46%. Thomson Reuters' tracking shows 41% of law firms actively using generative AI, with over 95% of legal professionals expecting it to become central to core workflows.

Two forces are pushing adoption forward:

  • Client pressure: 78% of corporate clients say AI-enabled quality is important or essential when choosing outside counsel, and 32% are actively reconsidering firms that lag on AI.
  • Time savings: Thomson Reuters estimates AI can save a legal professional roughly 240 hours per year — about six weeks of work.

The result is a profession split in two: teams using AI to cut the grunt work, and teams still doing it by hand. The gap compounds quickly.

Task 1: Legal research with real citations

Instead of keyword-searching a document set and reading hits, lawyers now ask questions in plain English: "What are my obligations under this agreement?" The AI searches the actual files, retrieves the relevant passages, and answers with the exact source — document, page, and passage — so you can verify before you rely on it. Enterprise tools like Lexis+ AI and Westlaw have made citation-grounded research the norm; research is already one of the two most-used AI applications in law firms, at roughly 73-74% usage.

Task 2: Document review and summarization

First-pass review of discovery, due diligence, and deal documents is prime AI territory. A legal AI reads every page and produces structured summaries, flags anomalies, and groups documents by topic — turning days of junior-associate time into minutes. This is the single most-deployed legal AI use case, at around 74-77% usage, led by e-discovery platforms like Relativity and Everlaw.

Task 3: Contract clause screening

AI contract review scans an agreement and flags the clauses that actually matter: unlimited liability, auto-renewal, broad indemnification, missing termination rights. Each finding is rated High / Medium / Low risk with a plain-English explanation. Contract lifecycle platforms like Luminance and Kira report up to 90% time savings on routine review — an 80-page master services agreement reviewed in minutes instead of hours.

Task 4: Due diligence on deal documents

During M&A and financing work, teams upload a data room and ask targeted questions: "Which contracts require change-of-control consent?" The AI searches the whole set, answers with citations, and lets the team verify each one — dramatically cutting the due diligence timeline. Harvey and similar enterprise platforms are widely deployed for exactly this in BigLaw transactions.

Task 5: Intake and matter triage

New client intake generates a flood of semi-structured documents. AI classifies them, extracts key facts (parties, dates, amounts), and drafts the intake summary — so a paralegal can spend time on the matter instead of data entry.

Task 6: Compliance checks against your own playbook

For repeatable work — jurisdictional rules, firm formatting standards, disclosure requirements — you define a playbook once and let the AI scan every document against it. Violations are flagged, rated, and explained automatically.

Task 7: First-draft memos and summaries

AI generates solid first drafts from your own documents — memos, chronologies, issue summaries — which a lawyer then reviews and refines. Drafting is now the fastest-growing legal AI category at roughly 50-59% usage. The key is that the draft is grounded in the source material, not hallucinated.

The accuracy caveat every lawyer must know

AI is powerful, but it is not infallible — and in legal work, a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. The landmark Stanford RegLab study of leading legal AI tools found that even specialized, retrieval-based legal AI systems produced incorrect or unsupported answers 17-33% of the time — and general-purpose models hallucinated at far higher rates.

The cautionary tale is now part of professional lore: in Mata v. Avianca (2023), two lawyers used ChatGPT to draft a brief, and it fabricated entire case citations that didn't exist. The court sanctioned them $5,000 — a reminder that AI output must always be verified against primary sources.

The practical rules:

  • Demand citations — every claim should point to a document, page, and passage you can open.
  • Verify before you rely — especially for anything going to a court or a client.
  • Use tools that can say "I don't know" — a legal AI that never admits it can't find an answer is a red flag.

The confidentiality question: where does your data go?

ABA Model Rule 1.6 requires lawyers to protect confidential client information — and ABA Formal Opinion 512 makes clear that duty extends to AI tools. Uploading client documents to a public or consumer cloud chatbot can implicate that duty, because many services retain or train on what you paste.

The safest architecture is local-first: the AI runs on your own machine, your documents never leave it, and nothing is retained by a third party. This is the standard we recommend for attorney–client privileged material.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will AI replace lawyers?

Not in the foreseeable future. AI automates specific tasks — research, document review, contract screening — but legal judgment, strategy, negotiation, and client relationships remain human work. The lawyers who adopt AI will outwork those who don't; the job changes, it doesn't disappear.

What legal tasks can AI automate today?

The most mature use cases are legal research with citations, document review and summarization, contract clause screening, due diligence, intake triage, and drafting first-pass memos. Document review and legal research are the most-used applications, and the ABA's 2024 survey found adoption at firms with 100+ attorneys rose from 16% to 46% between 2023 and 2024.

Is it safe to use AI for client work?

Yes, if you choose privacy-first tools. Cloud chatbots may train on or retain what you paste, which can implicate ABA Model Rule 1.6 confidentiality duties. Local-first apps like Lawyer Assistant keep documents on your machine — nothing leaves your computer in local mode — which is why they're recommended for confidential client work.

How do lawyers and paralegals get started with AI?

Start with one high-volume, low-risk task such as searching your own document set, then expand. Pick a tool with citations you can verify, run it on a pilot matter, and check every answer. ABA Formal Opinion 512 requires lawyers to understand their AI tools' risks and verify outputs before relying on them — the recommended starting point is a private, local legal AI app that reads your existing files.
